Build an SLP-guided home-practice app that combines strict phoneme-level audio scoring with selective, evidence-limited visual feedback—then earn broader claims through staged clinical validation.

Executive decision. Do not launch as an autonomous diagnostic product. Launch first as a clinician-configured practice assistant for children with selected speech sound errors. 1. Product thesis and boundaries

The opportunity is not “speech-to-text for children.” Generic recognition systems are intentionally tolerant of accents, noisy speech, and likely intent; that makes them poorly suited to deciding whether a target phoneme was produced. The app should instead compare a short, known prompt against a constrained set of expected and clinically relevant alternate pronunciations, align the utterance at the phoneme level, and use video only when it can add reliable evidence.

Success definition

  • A learner can complete a 5–8 minute assigned session without a caregiver troubleshooting the technology.
  • The system correctly passes clear productions, catches clinically meaningful target errors, and abstains when signal quality or model certainty is inadequate.
  • Feedback is actionable, brief, age-appropriate, and never more specific than the evidence supports.
  • An SLP can assign targets, review trends and representative attempts, correct model labels, and decide the next clinical step.
  • No subgroup is silently subjected to materially worse false-rejection or false-acceptance rates.

Explicit non-goals for the first release

  • No diagnosis, disorder classification, treatment-plan generation, or replacement for an SLP.
  • No analysis of free conversation, real-time surveillance, emotion inference, or facial identity recognition.
  • No confident inference about articulators the front camera cannot see, such as tongue-to-palate contact for many productions.
  • No open-ended generative clinical advice. Feedback comes from an SLP-authored, versioned library with model-selected parameters.
  • No advertising, data brokerage, third-party behavioral tracking, or secondary model training without separate informed consent.

2. Clinical model

Speech sound disorders can involve perception, motor production, phonological representation, or a combination. A single observed error does not establish its cause. The product therefore separates attempt scoring from clinical interpretation: the app reports what it observed and offers a practice cue; the SLP owns diagnosis, target selection, and treatment decisions.

Clinical governance

  • Form a paid clinical advisory group with at least three practicing pediatric SLPs, one speech motor/acoustics researcher, one multilingual/multidialectal specialist, and one caregiver advocate.
  • Name a clinical safety lead with release-blocking authority over feedback content, target eligibility, and model performance thresholds.
  • Maintain a “claim ledger” mapping every user-facing statement to its supporting sensor evidence, validation result, intended population, and contraindications.
  • Require two-SLP approval for new cue templates and target-sound protocols; measure inter-rater agreement on annotation tasks.
  • Use establishment → generalization → maintenance as the practice progression, with SLP-configured mastery criteria rather than a universal gamified score.

Clinical language rule. The app may say “I heard…,” “I saw…,” “try…,” or “I couldn’t get a clear read.” It may not say “you have…,” “the cause is…,” or assert invisible anatomy. Repeated mismatches trigger clinician review, not increasingly confident automated diagnosis.

3. Core user experience

Clinician setup

  1. Create or import a learner profile using the minimum necessary information; record language/dialect context, age band, target inventory, cue preferences, and accessibility needs.
  2. Select an evidence-based practice structure and assign targets by sound, word position, complexity, and context. The initial content library is curated, not generated on demand.
  3. Set the session dose, retry limit, whether video is enabled for each target, and which attempts may be saved for review.
  4. Share a caregiver invitation with verifiable consent and a plain-language explanation of microphone, camera, storage, and research choices.

Learner session

  1. Warm-up: check microphone, background noise, face framing, and permission state without recording to the server.
  2. Prompt: show a culturally reviewed image, play an optional SLP-recorded model, and highlight the target sound without requiring reading.
  3. Attempt: capture a short utterance with a visible recording state and immediate stop control.
  4. Quality decision: if noise, clipping, occlusion, multiple speakers, or poor framing prevents a fair assessment, ask for a neutral retry and do not count it as an error.
  5. Fast path: a confidently correct target receives brief positive feedback; skip deep video analysis and advance.
  6. Uncertain path: an ambiguous score receives one specific capture suggestion. After two uncertain attempts, offer a model/rehearsal or move on.
  7. Analysis path: a confident target mismatch invokes only the sound-specific visual feature extractor that has been validated for that prompt.
  8. Feedback: give one cue at a time, demonstrate it visually, and allow a comparison attempt. Stop after the configured retry budget to prevent frustration.
  9. Close: celebrate effort and consistency, not “perfect speech,” and show a short caregiver summary without clinical overinterpretation.

4. Intelligent processing pipeline

Why ordinary speech-to-text is not the gate

The first model should be a constrained pronunciation scorer. It receives the expected word, target phoneme, word position, acceptable language/dialect variants, and a curated list of plausible substitutions, omissions, and distortions. It produces phoneme timings, posterior scores, quality measures, and a calibrated outcome—not merely a transcript. A general ASR transcript can be retained as a weak supporting feature, never as the sole correctness decision.

if capture_quality < quality_floor:
    return RETRY_CAPTURE(reason)

audio = score_expected_pronunciation(
    signal,
    expected_phonemes,
    allowed_variants,
    clinically_relevant_error_set
)

if audio.p_correct >= pass_threshold and audio.is_calibrated:
    return CORRECT(skip_deep_vision=True)

if audio.is_ambiguous or audio.p_error < analysis_threshold:
    return RETRY_OR_ABSTAIN

if not target.visual_protocol_validated or not video_consent:
    return AUDIO_CUE_OR_CLINICIAN_REVIEW

visual = analyze_supported_cues(video_features, target.protocol)
result = calibrated_fusion(audio, visual)
return APPROVED_TEMPLATE(result) if result.confident else ABSTAIN

Threshold philosophy

  • Optimize separately for false passes (a true target error marked correct) and false rejects (an acceptable production marked wrong). A single “accuracy” number hides the tradeoff.
  • Use a three-way decision—pass, analyze, abstain—not a forced binary. The ambiguous middle band protects learners from arbitrary correction.
  • Calibrate by target sound, word position, age band, device family, and validated language/dialect. Do not copy one threshold across all contexts.
  • Set launch thresholds with SLPs from held-out, speaker-independent data. Product managers cannot loosen them to improve completion metrics without clinical approval.
  • Measure the routing rate. The deep path should be uncommon because success, poor capture, and uncertainty do not need an expensive multimodal model.

5. Model and data strategy

Build-versus-buy recommendation

Buy commodity infrastructure—secure storage, authentication, notifications, observability—and prototype acoustic encoders or landmark extraction with reputable existing models. Build the clinically meaningful layers: prompt lexicon, phoneme alignment and error hypotheses, calibration, sound-specific visual protocols, multimodal fusion, feedback policy, and evaluation harness. The differentiated asset is the consented, expertly annotated attempt dataset and the evidence linking signals to safe feedback.

Dataset program

  1. Define the sampling frame. For every target, balance correct productions and clinically relevant errors across word positions, vowel contexts, severity, age band, device, lighting/noise, skin tone, facial characteristics, and the validated language/dialect population.
  2. Collect ethically. Use IRB review when the work is human-subjects research or intended for publication/generalizable knowledge. Obtain parental permission and age-appropriate assent. Separate product consent from optional research contribution.
  3. Annotate in layers. Capture prompt, phoneme boundaries, perceptual correctness, error type, confidence, audio quality, visual observability, visible cue states, and “cannot determine.” Never force annotators to infer hidden anatomy.
  4. Measure label quality. Double-label high-risk samples, adjudicate disagreements, report inter-rater reliability by target and subgroup, and preserve the original ratings.
  5. Split by speaker and household. Prevent the same child, sibling, session, or near-duplicate clip from leaking across train, calibration, and test sets.
  6. Maintain challenge sets. Include background voices, whispering, braces, missing teeth, face coverings, low light, head turn, fast speech, emotional speech, and acceptable dialectal variants.
  7. Version everything. Dataset cards record consent scope, provenance, collection protocol, exclusions, transformations, label policy, subgroup coverage, known limitations, and deletion lineage.

Model release controls

  • Model registry with dataset, code, hyperparameters, intended use, exclusions, metrics, subgroup slices, reviewer, and rollback target.
  • Shadow evaluation before user-facing rollout; feature flag by target protocol and population.
  • Drift monitoring on capture quality, score distributions, routing, abstention, SLP overrides, devices, and subgroup deltas—never on raw media dashboards.
  • No unsupervised online learning from children’s attempts. Corrections enter a governed labeling queue and a later versioned training run.
  • Every result stores the model, lexicon, threshold, and feedback-template versions needed to reproduce the decision.

5A. Open-source model landscape and train-your-own decision

Recommendation: open-source foundation, proprietary clinical layer. Do not train a speech foundation model from scratch and do not ship an off-the-shelf pronunciation score. Benchmark several permissively licensed encoders, fine-tune the best one on consented child speech, train target-specific scoring/calibration heads and small visual-cue models, and distill the winning path for the phone. This captures most of the benefit of open research while keeping the clinically meaningful behavior under your control.

How candidates are judged

A model’s general word-error rate is not the deciding metric. Score each codebase, checkpoint, and dataset separately; “open-source code” does not guarantee commercially usable weights or training data.

Recommended hybrid model architecture

  1. Audio encoder bake-off: wav2vec 2.0 Base/XLSR, WavLM Base/Base+, and a compact NeMo Conformer-CTC. Freeze encoders first; compare target-aligned embeddings and CTC posteriors. Include Whisper and GOPT only as reference baselines.
  2. Constrained hypothesis layer: compile expected, acceptable-variant, deletion, and substitution phone paths from a versioned clinical lexicon. Score those paths without a broad word language model.
  3. Owned clinical heads: train a small target/error classifier, capture-quality classifier, and calibrator on your consented child data. Produce pass/analyze/abstain plus an evidence vector.
  4. Owned visual path: normalize MediaPipe landmarks by head pose and facial scale; compute external mouth features; align them to audio phone spans; train a separate tiny temporal head per cue family.
  5. Rule-constrained fusion: visual evidence can strengthen or weaken only claims approved for that target. It cannot override poor capture or manufacture evidence about an invisible articulator.
  6. Distillation: after the server model passes, distill into a compact CTC/embedding student and quantize to Core ML. Revalidate calibration, target metrics, and subgroup slices on real devices.

Data and compute planning assumptions

  • Do not promise model quality from a universal sample-size heuristic. Construct learning curves per target and stop collecting when the confidence interval and subgroup coverage—not merely loss—support the intended claim.
  • For a narrow frozen-encoder feasibility study, plan on roughly 10k–30k adjudicated attempts across at least 200 children, with enough true examples of each clinically relevant error; this is a collection target to test, not a guarantee of adequacy.
  • Full encoder adaptation and robust multi-target calibration will likely require tens to hundreds of thousands of attempts plus additional consented unlabeled child audio. Rare distortions and subgroup coverage, not overall clip count, will be the bottleneck.
  • Every target needs independent speakers in train, calibration, and locked test sets. Reserve a prospective clinic/device cohort that is never used for model or threshold selection.
  • Budget annotation as a first-class expense: SLP double labels, adjudication, visibility labels, disagreement retention, and clinical-content review will cost more than many initial GPU runs.

Open-source intake checklist

  • Record repository commit, package version, checkpoint hash, model card, code license, weight license, training datasets, dataset licenses, acceptable-use terms, and any click-through agreement.
  • Distinguish research access from commercial training, embedding, redistribution, mobile bundling, and production inference rights.
  • Scan dependencies and transitive native libraries; generate an SBOM and attribution file; isolate copyleft research tools from shipped proprietary code.
  • Do not upload child media to public model hubs, hosted demos, issue trackers, or third-party telemetry while evaluating open models.
  • Treat checkpoint conversion, quantization, fine-tuning, and distillation as derivative-work questions requiring counsel, not as automatic ways around source terms.
  • Re-review licenses and model cards at every upgrade. A familiar project name can publish checkpoints under different terms.

7. Privacy, security, accessibility, and regulatory workstream

Regulatory claim strategy

Commission U.S. regulatory counsel during discovery. FDA’s January 2026 general-wellness guidance distinguishes low-risk lifestyle functions from software intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, prevent, or treat a disease or condition; this concept is explicitly therapeutic, so marketing language alone may not keep it outside device oversight. FDA’s January 2026 clinical decision support guidance also notes that policies for patient/caregiver software functions still apply. Produce a documented intended-use and classification assessment before the pilot, then repeat it whenever claims, population, or autonomy expand.

  • Phase 1 claim: helps users practice clinician-selected speech targets and records progress; not a diagnosis or substitute for professional care.
  • Do not use “detects the cause,” “treats speech disorders,” “clinical-grade,” or accuracy claims until counsel and supporting evidence approve them.
  • Maintain design history, hazards, verification evidence, model change controls, complaint handling, and incident procedures from the start, even if the initial conclusion is non-device.
  • If the product later independently recommends treatment or claims to diagnose/mitigate a disorder, initiate the appropriate medical-device quality and submission strategy before release.

Child and health data

  • The initial pilot is U.S.-only. Complete separate GDPR, UK children’s code, and other local child/health/biometric analyses before international expansion.
  • COPPA analysis is mandatory for under-13 users. The FTC’s 2025 final amendments expand personal information to include biometric identifiers and add protections around children’s data disclosures.
  • HIPAA depends on relationships and use cases, not whether data “feels medical.” A direct-to-consumer app may not be a HIPAA covered entity, while an app operating on behalf of a clinic may be a business associate requiring BAAs and HIPAA safeguards.
  • For non-HIPAA health apps, assess the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule, which was updated to clarify coverage of health apps and similar technologies.
  • Map FERPA, COPPA school-consent mechanics, and applicable state student-record, biometric, consumer-health, breach, retention, and consent laws before selecting pilot states or school partners.

Privacy-by-design requirements

  • Just-in-time camera and microphone explanations; separate toggles for practice processing, clinician review, and research contribution.
  • Video off by default when it cannot improve a validated target. No background capture and no facial templates used for identity.
  • Ephemeral raw video by default. If a clinician sample is saved, show the exact retention period and who can view it.
  • Self-service caregiver access, export, revocation, and deletion; operational deletion SLA plus model/dataset lineage review.
  • No advertising SDKs, session replay, cross-app identifiers, or analytics tools that receive health/context data.
  • Vendor contracts prohibit secondary use and model training, specify breach notice, deletion, subprocessors, geography, and audit rights.

Security and accessibility baseline

  • Threat-model child account takeover, clinic tenant leakage, media URL leakage, insecure device storage, prompt/content tampering, model extraction, consent mismatch, and insider access.
  • Use passkeys or strong MFA for clinicians, least-privilege roles, short sessions for privileged actions, tenant-scoped authorization tests, encryption, key management, immutable audit events, dependency scanning, secrets scanning, and incident drills.
  • Verify the mobile client against relevant OWASP MASVS groups and remote services against a web/API security standard; commission independent penetration testing before a clinical pilot.
  • Target WCAG 2.2 AA for the portal and equivalent native accessibility: VoiceOver, Dynamic Type, reduced motion, high contrast, captions, non-audio instructions, large targets, switch access, and plain-language caregiver content.
  • Because users have speech disabilities, never make speech the only way to navigate, request help, authenticate, or correct the system.

Evaluation design

  1. Offline analytic validation: locked, speaker-held-out test set; compare audio-only, visual-only, and fused systems. Report confidence intervals and full target/subgroup slices.
  2. Wizard-of-Oz formative study: 12–20 learner/caregiver dyads and 5–8 SLPs test capture instructions, cue comprehension, retry behavior, consent, and emotional response before model automation.
  3. Prospective silent pilot: models score attempts but do not show corrective feedback; SLPs independently label a sample to measure real-world agreement and drift.
  4. Controlled usability/feasibility pilot: approximately 30–60 learners across several clinicians for 6–8 weeks, with adverse-feedback review and weekly model-safety monitoring.
  5. Clinical utility study: pre-register a protocol comparing standard home practice with app-assisted practice, use blinded outcome scoring where possible, and distinguish target-word improvement from generalization to untrained speech.

Sample sizes above are planning ranges, not statistical commitments. A biostatistician should power each study from the chosen primary endpoint, clustering, baseline variability, expected attrition, and clinically meaningful effect.

Safety indicators that override growth metrics

  • Unsupported physical-cause statement shown to a learner.
  • Acceptable dialectal or multilingual production repeatedly labeled as an error.
  • Material subgroup disparity in pass, reject, quality, or abstention behavior.
  • Repeated correction loop beyond the configured retry budget.
  • Raw child media retained, viewed, or used outside the captured consent purpose.
  • Clinician override or complaint cluster tied to a model, target, prompt, or device version.

First-release backlog, in order

  1. Clinical target protocol and content schema.
  2. Consent, assent, identity, roles, retention, export, and deletion.
  3. Capture-quality coaching with no scoring.
  4. Audio-only prompt, attempt, alignment, pass/analyze/abstain, and reproducible evaluation.
  5. Clinician assignment and review with overrides.
  6. One externally visible cue family behind a research flag.
  7. Selective routing, approved feedback templates, and retry safeguards.
  8. Safety, equity, security, accessibility, and operations gates.

What not to build early

  • A large game world, avatar marketplace, streak pressure, social leaderboard, or parent growth dashboard.
  • A broad word library before the phonetic contexts and annotations are validated.
  • A general conversational agent that invents exercises or clinical explanations.
  • School/EHR integrations before consent, tenancy, exports, and deletion are dependable.
  • Android parity before the synchronized capture and test harness work reliably on a controlled iOS device set.

13. Verification and release gates

Engineering

  • Automated unit, schema, authorization, tenant-isolation, consent-policy, deletion, model-routing, threshold-version, and feedback-template tests.
  • Golden synchronized audio/video fixtures across supported devices; deterministic replay through every released model.
  • Offline/slow-network, interrupted upload, permission denial, camera disabled, multiple speakers, background noise, long content, and accessibility flows.
  • Load, latency, cost, rollback, data-restoration, disaster-recovery, audit-log integrity, dependency, static, dynamic, and penetration tests.

Clinical and model

  • Locked evaluation report per target with false-pass/reject, calibration, abstention, error confusion, confidence intervals, and subgroup slices.
  • Evidence that fused analysis adds value over the audio baseline; otherwise keep the simpler model.
  • Clinical review of every prompt, image, model recording, cue, reinforcement message, contraindication, and escalation route.
  • Audit a statistically justified sample of complete result-to-feedback traces; no unsupported claim reaches release.

Privacy, safety, and operations

  • Data protection impact assessment, data-flow map, vendor inventory, consent records, retention schedule, deletion proof, incident and breach playbooks.
  • Regulatory intended-use/classification memo and approved claim set.
  • WCAG 2.2 AA audit for the portal plus native assistive-technology testing with disabled users.
  • Clinical safety case signed by the clinical lead; privacy/security sign-off; support and complaint owners trained.
  • Feature flags, target-level kill switches, last-known-good model, and a tested rollback that does not corrupt attempt history.
Go/no-go rule. A phase advances only when its evidence package passes. Schedule pressure, model novelty, partner enthusiasm, or aggregate accuracy cannot waive a failed target, safety, consent, security, accessibility, or subgroup gate.

14. The first 30 days

  1. Appoint product, clinical safety, technical, and privacy owners; execute advisor agreements.
  2. Interview 8–10 SLPs, 6–8 caregivers, and 4–6 learners; observe current home-practice handoffs and breakdowns.
  3. Write the intended-use statement, excluded populations, pilot claim set, and initial regulatory questions for counsel.
  4. Select two audio targets and one visually observable cue family using clinical value, observability, data feasibility, and equity risk—not demo appeal.
  5. Draft the prompt/variant/error lexicon, annotation guide, claim ledger, retry policy, and SLP-authored feedback examples.
  6. Prototype on-device capture-quality coaching and run a small device/lighting/noise feasibility session before training a visual model.
  7. Define the consent/data lifecycle and vendor requirements; decide what never leaves the phone.
  8. Specify the locked evaluation sets, advancement metrics, subgroup plan, and prospective silent-pilot protocol.
  9. Build a clickable learner flow and clinician assignment/review flow; test comprehension and emotional tone.
  10. At day 30, hold a go/no-go review on the narrow vertical slice and publish the approved target protocol.
